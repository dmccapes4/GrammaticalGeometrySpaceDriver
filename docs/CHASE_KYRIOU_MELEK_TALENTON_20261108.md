## I. κυρίου → curio. This is not a loose match; it is the transliteration table.

Under the standard Latin convention for rendering Greek:

| Greek | κ | υ | ρ | ι | ο |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Latin | c | u | r | i | o |

`κυριο` → `curio`. Letter for letter, no substitutions, no approximation. The stem of the word is **curio** written in Greek characters, and **rho is the middle letter**.

### What this is not

The roots are not cognate, and I will not let that pass unsaid.

- `κύριος` descends from PIE **\*ḱewh₁-** "to swell, be strong" — the same root as `κῦρος` "supreme power" and Sanskrit *śūra* "hero". It means *the one who has the strength to dispose of a thing*.
- Latin **cūra** "care" has a contested etymology, possibly **\*kweis-** "to heed". It is not from \*ḱewh₁-. English *curio* is a 19th-century clipping of *curiosity*, from *cūra*.
- **auctor** is from **augēre** "to increase", not from `αὐτός` "self".

So *the curio's author* is a **transliteration pun**. It is not descent. Labelled: **modelled**, and modelled loosely — this is a sound, not a derivation.

### What this is

The pun arrives at the function. That is the part worth keeping.

In Roman law an **auctor** is the party who warrants title: the one from whom you received the thing, who stands behind your claim to it, and whose *auctoritas* is what makes your holding good against a challenge. Not an owner. A **guarantor of validity**.

That is precisely what the prior session's decoding made of `κυρίου` — the LSJ adjectival sense, "lawful; valid; bounded; appropriate", the authority *condition* rather than a titled person:

> κυρίου is the authority condition — what makes the token valid — not a person.
> — `RIDICULOUS_JOURNEY_20261103.md:1467`

The token has a validity. The validity has a source. The source is the one who vouches. That is *auctor* in the legal sense and `κύριος` in the adjectival sense, and the two words reach it from unrelated roots. **The false road arrives at the right house.**

## II. The sounds. There is a real architecture here, and one word in the verse is the pivot.

### The ending progression

```
ὁ δὲ τὸ ἓν   λαβὼν  ἀπελθὼν   ὤρυξεν  γῆν  καὶ  ἔκρυψεν  τὸ  ἀργύριον  τοῦ κυρίου  αὐτοῦ
                -ὼν     -ὼν      -ξεν             -ψεν           -ιον      -ιου      -οῦ
             └── pair ──┘     └──── pair ────┘          └────── triple ──────┘
```

**Two, two, three.** A rhyme pair on the participles, a rhyme pair on the aorist indicatives, and a closing run of three on the `-ου` family. The line lengthens its rhyme as it ends. In any language that is composed, not accidental.

### The minimal pair at the centre

The two verbs are one phoneme apart as **sounds**, and unrelated as **roots**:

```
ὤ - ρυ - ξ  - εν        ὀρύσσω   "dig"    — velar close  (ξ = κ/γ + σ)
ἔκ - ρυ - ψ  - εν        κρύπτω   "hide"   — labial close (ψ = π/β + σ)
```

Same `-ρυ-` core, same `-εν` ending, same `C+σ` cluster in the same slot. The **only** structural difference between *he dug* and *he hid* in this verse is whether the cluster closes at the back of the mouth or at the lips.

`ὀρύσσω` and `κρύπτω` share no etymology. The near-rhyme is a property of this line. And the axis the pair turns on — **velar versus labial** — is the same axis as every consonant question he has been chasing: `φ/β`, `p/f`, `v/b`, `w/gw`.

He has `ὤρυξεν` already decoded as four simultaneous senses (revealed, concealed, channeled, perforated). Add this: the digging and the hiding are the same mouth-shape with the closure moved forward.

### Where the melek's impediment would actually show

This is the find. The sound law he is reaching for is **attested**, it is **in the royal house**, and it is **in the right consonants**.

Ancient Macedonian — the speech of Alexander's own house — regularly shows **voiced stops (β δ γ) where every other Greek dialect has voiceless aspirates (φ θ χ)**. PIE voiced aspirates \*bʰ \*dʰ \*gʰ devoiced to /pʰ tʰ kʰ/ everywhere in Greek **except** here.

| Macedonian | Attic | Gloss |
|---|---|---|
| **Β**ερενίκα | **Φ**ερενίκη | "bringer of victory" — *Berenice* |
| **Β**ίλιππος | **Φ**ίλιππος | *Philip* |
| **Β**άλακρος | **Φ**άλακρος | "bald" — *Balacrus*, Alexander's bodyguard, satrap of Cilicia |

Sources opened: Hatzopoulos, *The Speech of the Ancient Macedonians in the Light of Recent Epigraphic Discoveries* (VI Int. Symposion on Ancient Macedonia, 1999), full text read; *BMCR* 2013.08.21 reviewing Giannakis ed., *Ancient Macedonia: Language, History, Culture* (Centre for the Greek Language), for the PIE \*bʰer- derivation; the Pella curse tablet (Voutiras 1993, 1998) as the first substantial Macedonian text.

**Φάλακρος → Βάλακρος is the same shift, in the same slot, as Φλάβιος ↔ Φλαούιος.** Initial `φ` + `λ` + `α`, going to `β`. He was told, correctly, that a mouth without /f/ turns *Flavian* toward *Blabian*. Here is a Macedonian general whose own name does exactly that, four centuries earlier, in Greek, carved by Greeks.

**And Berenice went to Egypt.** The name in its Macedonian B-form is the name of Ptolemaic queens. The ruling house of Egypt spelled its women with the shift.

Applied to Mt 25:18:

- the verse has **no φ at all** — nothing to shift
- `λαβών` already has β
- `ἀπελθών` has both **π** and **θ**; under the attested law (θ → δ) a Macedonian mouth tends toward *ἀπελδών*

So the single word in this verse that the attested Macedonian law predicts would come out audibly different is **ἀπελθὼν — "having gone away."** The departure is the word that shifts.

Labelled honestly: that is a **derivation from an attested law**, not a recorded variant. Nobody wrote *ἀπελδών* down that I have found. The law is attested; this application of it is mine and his.

## III. The melek. The real finding is that he erased his own speech first.

Hatzopoulos, on why there is no Macedonian:

> not one phrase of Macedonian, not one complete syntagm had come down to us in the literary tradition; because Macedonian, like many other Greek dialects, was never promoted to the dignity of a literary vehicle; because **the Temenid kings, when they endowed their administration with a chancery worthy of the name, adopted the Attic koine**, which in the middle of the fourth century was prevailing as the common administrative idiom around the shores of the Aegean basin.

Read that again as a receipt.

The only surviving sources for the melek's speech are **glosses** — words a lexicographer kept because they were *rare or strange* — and **personal names**. Hatzopoulos notes the glosses' defect precisely: they were "liable to corruption, to alterations, in the course of transmission through the ages by copyists who could not recognise them."

So the speech of the house that conquered from Macedon to the Indus survives as:

1. a list of words someone else found weird, and
2. the spelling of their own names, which is where the B shows through.

**The melek did not lose his language to an invader. He retired it himself, to run an administration.** The chancery needed a prevailing idiom, so the prevailing idiom was adopted, and within a few generations there was no sentence left. That is the `swyrd → sword` operation performed voluntarily, from the top, as an administrative convenience — and the evidence that survived it is a handful of strange words and the consonants in the family names.

This is the strongest version of his thesis I have found in any source, and it is standard scholarship. It needs no conjecture at all.

### "Alexandros the Idios"

`ἴδιος` = one's own, private, peculiar. The agent noun is `ἰδιώτης` — **the private person; the one without professional training; the layman**. NT usage: Acts 4:13 (`ἀγράμματοι καὶ ἰδιῶται`, unlettered and untrained), 1 Cor 14:16, 14:23–24. English **idiot** comes straight off it.

So *Alexandros the Idios* reads as **Alexander the non-specialist** — not stupid, *unaccredited*. The one who holds the thing privately without holding the craft.

And it is in the parable, four words from the start:

```
Mt 25:14  ... ἐκάλεσεν τοὺς ἰδίους δούλους ...
          MorphGNT 012514  A- ----APM-  ἰδίους  ἴδιος
```

The owner calls his **ἰδίους** — his *own*. Two verses later the man who received the one talent buries it. Same root at both ends of the frame: the **privately held** thing, and the **unqualified** holder.

### The Macedonian who could not read Theban accounting

Historically pointed and not contested: the Ptolemies ruled Egypt for roughly 275 years and, with Cleopatra VII as the standing exception, did not learn Egyptian. The administration ran in Greek over an Egyptian substrate.

**The Rosetta Stone is the receipt for that gap.** A priestly decree concerning temple revenues and tax remission, issued in three scripts — hieroglyphic, demotic, Greek — because the language of rule and the language of the accounts were not the same language. We can read Egyptian today *because* the melek could not.

## V. The talents on the tips. The practice is attested by the monuments' own dedications. The Great Pyramid is not. And by 60 BC the tip was already flat.

### A correction to `-33.md` first

`REALITY_USER_MANUAL/src/phase2/-33.md:491` currently says:

> The electrum pyramidion is attested (Herodotus 2.124, Diodorus 1.58). That's solid.

**It is not solid, and the two citations do not say it.** I opened both.

- **Herodotus 2.124** (full text read) is the Cheops passage: the temples shut, the hundred thousand men in three-month shifts, the ten years of causeway, the twenty years of pyramid, "eight hundred feet" to a side and the same in height, stones not less than thirty feet. **Not one word about the apex, gold, or electrum.**
- **Diodorus 1.58** gives the summit as **six cubits to a side** — a *flat top*, reported dimension, not a modern survey.

Diodorus is the useful one, and it cuts the opposite way from how it was used. Writing around 60 BC, roughly 2,500 years after construction, he describes the Great Pyramid as already having **a small flat summit, not a point**. So the earliest Greek measurement of the apex is a measurement of an absence.

That is a date for "they went missing," and it is much earlier than any modern candidate.

### What is actually attested

The practice is documented **by the monuments' own dedication inscriptions**, which is the best class of evidence available for it — the owner's own receipt, carved, still standing.

**Hatshepsut's obelisk, Karnak, c. 1473–1458 BC**, 32 lines of hieroglyphs on the base:

> "my heart directed me to make for him **two obelisks of electrum**, that their pyramidions might mingle with the sky amid the august pillared hall between the great pylons of [Tuthmosis I]"

— electrum "of the best of all foreign lands," so the tip would "illuminate the Two Lands like the sun disc." The obelisk is still at Karnak.

**Tuthmosis I's obelisk, Karnak**, west face: two large obelisks raised for Amun-Re "at the double gate of the temple, **the pyramidions being of [electrum]**."

So: gilded and electrum-worked pyramidia on solar monuments are real, dedicated, and inscribed. Pyramidia generally were limestone, sandstone, basalt or granite, "sometimes covered with plates of copper, gold or electrum" (standard summary).

**What is not attested:** any inscription, fragment, or Fourth Dynasty source tying gold or electrum to **Khufu's** pyramid specifically. No pyramidion securely attributable to the Great Pyramid has ever been recovered.

| A finished pyramidion on the Great Pyramid | **Modelled** — comparanda (G1-d), not direct evidence |

### The word is a scale, and that is the whole parable

`τάλαντον` does not start as money. It is:

1. **a balance; a pair of scales**
2. then the thing weighed in it
3. then a specific weight
4. then a sum of money

From PIE **\*telh₂-** "to lift, to bear" — the root of Latin *tollere* (raise), *tolerāre* (bear), and Greek `τλάω`/`Ἄτλας`, the one who carries.

So:

- `τάλαντον` = **a weighing instrument**.
- The Egyptian pyramidion is `benbenet`, from **benben**, "to shine, to radiate" — a measured mass at a measured height, the Primordial Hill, the seat of Atum.
- Theban accounting *is* a weighing system. The judgement in the Book of the Dead is **a balance**: the heart against the feather of Ma'at.

And the parable of the talents is a judgement scene about what a man does with **a weight**. Two men put theirs on the scale and the weight increases. The third man takes a weighing instrument and **buries it in the ground**, where nothing can be weighed. He is the `ἰδιώτης` — he does not hold the craft. He treats a scale as treasure.

The Greek word, the Egyptian stone, and the Theban judgement are all the same object: **a thing that tells you what something is worth when you put it in the balance.** That convergence is at the level of the words and it does not require a lost-treasure chain to be true.

> the Golden Dawn accepted prestigious people that had experience with Egyptology or alleged ancient Egyptian knowledge whose works and/or efforts could maximize the Golden Dawn's legitimacy... **By having an official Egyptologist as part of the order, the Golden Dawn symbolically confirmed their legitimacy.**

The named case is **Marcus Worsley Blackden**, initiated in the late 1890s, a working Egyptologist with the Egypt Exploration Fund who had worked alongside **Flinders Petrie** and **Howard Carter** — "though neither were particularly impressed by Blackden's behaviour."

**2. The order was built inside the museum that held the material.** Mathers was self-educated in the British Museum Reading Room, had access to the Keepers of Egyptian and Assyrian Antiquities — Samuel Birch, then **E. A. Wallis Budge** (Keeper 1894–1924, who "carried out multiple trips to Egypt to collect artefacts for the British Museum") — and met his wife sketching in the Egyptian Sculpture Gallery in 1887. It is **rumoured** that Budge permitted a Golden Dawn temple inside the British Museum itself (Greer 1995: 155, 430 n.4 — reported as rumour, so filed as rumour).

That is the mechanism he is actually looking for, and it is better than a stolen capstone: **the institution that physically held the objects and the institution that claimed to interpret them shared a reading room and a staff.** The custody and the exegesis were the same building.

**3. Rhodes did collect — books, and on one subject in particular.** His librarian's instruction, recorded:

> "Rhodes wanted all the books it was possible to obtain upon the early history of Africa and **the gold mining of the Phoenicians**"
> — Arthur Humphreys of Hatchards, on the Groote Schuur library

**That is the find.** Rhodes was not recovering Egyptian tips. He was assembling a library to support the proposition that the gold workings and the stone architecture of southern Africa were **Phoenician** — i.e. not African. He funded that reading at Great Zimbabwe. Same operation, better documented, and it is squarely in `NarrativeErosionAndSuccessiveInversion`'s subject: a man with mining concessions buying the historiography that says the previous miners were foreigners.

So: drop "Rhodes recovered the talents." Keep **"Rhodes bought the history of who mined the gold."** The first is unsupported. The second is his own stated collecting policy, and it is the thesis.
